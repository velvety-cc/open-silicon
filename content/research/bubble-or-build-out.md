## Introduction

Few questions in technology are argued as loudly as whether AI infrastructure is a bubble. Both sides can point to real numbers. Usage and revenue are rising quickly. So are capital spending, borrowing and the commitments that sit outside company balance sheets.

For anyone lending against GPUs, the debate is not academic. It reaches a loan through the price of compute, the value of the hardware, the strength of the customers and the availability of refinancing. Getting the answer wrong in either direction is costly: too much caution forgoes sound loans, and too little leaves capital exposed when conditions turn.

Our thesis is that both cases rest on real evidence. Demand is turning into revenue, while spending and borrowing are growing faster than proven profits, and some of the money circulates among a small group of firms. For a lender, the useful question is narrower than "bubble or not": does this asset repay before its contract, or its usefulness, runs out?

We develop that view in seven steps. We first explain why a lender's question differs from an equity investor's. We then make the strongest bull case, that demand is real and already paying, followed by the strongest bear case, that spending, borrowing and the links between companies have run ahead of it. A look at the telecom boom of the late 1990s shows where losses landed last time and what is different now. That history points to a distinction the debate often misses, between debt on buildings and debt on chips. We close with the indicators we watch and the lending principles that follow.

The evidence is public and available as of mid-October 2026. This is market commentary, not investment advice. We start with the question a lender actually needs to answer.

## A lender asks a narrower question than an investor

The bubble debate is usually framed for equity investors, and that framing does not fit lending well. Equity investors debate valuation: whether today's share prices already assume more profit than the industry will earn. Lenders ask something more specific. Will this borrower repay this loan, from this asset, within this term?

The two questions can have different answers. A sector can be overvalued and still contain sound loans. It can be fairly valued and still contain bad ones. The difference is that a lender's upside is capped at being repaid, so what matters is not how good things could get but how bad they could get within the life of the loan.

That makes the bubble debate relevant to lenders mainly through four channels: the price of compute, the value of the hardware, the strength of the customers and whether refinancing will be available when it is needed. With those channels in mind, it is worth hearing each side at its strongest, starting with the bulls.

## The bull case: demand is real and already paying

The case for the build-out rests on more than enthusiasm. Its core claim is that demand for compute is already showing up in revenue, contracts, occupancy and prices.

**Usage is growing and turning into revenue.** OpenAI reported API usage of 6 billion tokens per minute at DevDay 2025 and more than 15 billion on March 31, 2026. Anthropic said in May 2026 that its annualized revenue run rate had crossed $47 billion, up from about $9 billion at the end of 2025. CNBC reported that the figure reached $65 billion by the end of July, and that OpenAI's annualized revenue was about $50 billion at the end of September. Run rates are not recognized revenue, but the direction is clear. Among GPU clouds, CoreWeave's second-quarter 2026 revenue of $2.575 billion was more than double the year before, and Nebius's AI cloud revenue rose 514%.

**Customers are signing long contracts.** Revenue today is matched by commitments for tomorrow. Microsoft reported that Azure and other cloud services revenue grew 43% in its June 2026 quarter. Its commercial remaining performance obligation, revenue under contract but not yet recognized, rose 84% to $678 billion.

**Capacity is tight.** Those commitments are filling the available space. JLL reported North American data center vacancy of about 1% in the first half of 2026, with 95% of the 66 gigawatts under construction already committed to tenants. CBRE reported that vacancy across 16 major markets fell from 8.3% to 6.7% in the year to the first quarter of 2026, even as capacity grew 25%. In April 2026, Anthropic said growth was straining its infrastructure and affecting reliability at peak hours.

**Rental prices recovered.** Tight capacity has shown up in what customers pay. The SemiAnalysis range for one-year H100 contracts fell to $1.45–1.95 per GPU-hour in October 2025, then recovered to $2.10–2.70 by April 2026. SemiAnalysis links the tightening to agents, open models and other inference demand, together with higher server costs and slower or cancelled deployments. Rising rents for a chip that has been shipping for more than three years are hard to square with a market full of idle capacity.

**Demand forecasts keep rising.** The bulls also point to what comes next. McKinsey's December 2025 model put AI data center demand at about 44 gigawatts in 2025 and 155.5 gigawatts in 2030, roughly 3.5 times as much, with inference growing faster than training.

**Financing is maturing.** Finally, capital markets are treating compute as a financeable asset. In March 2026, CoreWeave closed what it describes as the first investment-grade-rated GPU-backed financing. Rated, repeatable financing is a sign of an asset class forming, not only of speculation.

Taken together, the bull case is not that AI is fashionable but that it is already paying, under contract, in tight markets. The bear case accepts most of these facts. It argues that they are not enough.

## The bear case: spending, borrowing and circularity have run ahead

The bears do not deny demand. Their claim is that spending, borrowing and the links between a few large companies have grown faster than the revenue that has to support them.

**Spending is running ahead of revenue and cash flow.** In 2024, Sequoia's David Cahn estimated that AI needed about $600 billion a year of end-user revenue to justify the infrastructure being built. By mid-2025 he put the figure at roughly $840 billion. Fast as it is growing, the two leading labs' combined run rate is still a fraction of that. Epoch AI's June 2026 analysis found that the five largest cloud builders' operating cash flow was growing about 23% a year while their cash capital spending grew about 70%. On those trends, spending overtakes cash flow around the third quarter of 2026, and for Oracle it already has. Morgan Stanley estimated in July 2025 that about $2.9 trillion would be spent on data centers globally from 2025 to 2028, and that about $1.5 trillion of it would have to come from outside capital, including roughly $800 billion from private credit.

**More of it is borrowed, and more of it sits outside the balance sheet.** That outside capital is already arriving. Bank of America counted $121 billion of US corporate bonds from the five largest cloud builders in 2025, against an average of $28 billion a year from 2020 to 2024. By August 20, 2026, J.P. Morgan Asset Management counted $219 billion of investment-grade bonds from hyperscalers in 2026 alone, including issues in other currencies. Other commitments do not appear as debt at all. Meta's Hyperion campus in Louisiana is owned by a joint venture in which funds managed by Blue Owl hold 80% and Meta 20%. About $27 billion of 6.581% notes due 2049 were issued to finance the project. Meta leases the campus for an initial four years, with options to extend to 20, and provides a residual value guarantee of about $28 billion, but it does not consolidate the venture. Leases signed but not yet started add more: Microsoft reported $329.1 billion of them at June 30, 2026, Oracle $288 billion at August 31 and Amazon $137.2 billion at June 30. All of this is disclosed, but balance sheets alone understate the commitments behind the build-out.

**Some of the money goes in circles.** Part of the demand is also financed by the suppliers themselves. In September 2025, NVIDIA signed a letter of intent to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI as capacity was deployed. Its February 2026 annual report said an agreement was still being finalized, with no assurance it would be completed. NVIDIA has agreed to buy CoreWeave's unsold capacity through April 2032, at an initial value of $6.3 billion, and bought a further $2 billion of CoreWeave shares in January 2026. In August 2026 it guaranteed leases, capped at $105 billion, at an Ohio campus that will host only NVIDIA hardware. AMD granted OpenAI warrants for up to 160 million AMD shares that vest as OpenAI deploys AMD chips. NVIDIA and Microsoft committed to invest up to $10 billion and $5 billion in Anthropic, which committed to buy $30 billion of Azure compute. Microsoft said in January 2026 that about 45% of its commercial backlog came from OpenAI. Vendor financing is common in young industries and does not mean demand is fake. It does tie suppliers, clouds and labs together, so a setback at one can travel quickly to the others.

**The hardware ages faster than the debt.** Whatever is financed, much of it is equipment with a short life. Hyperscalers lengthened the accounting lives of their servers to five or six years over the past several years. Amazon shortened some back to five in 2025, and CoreWeave depreciates its data center computing equipment over six. In November 2025, Michael Burry argued that these longer lives would understate the industry's depreciation by about $176 billion over 2026–2028. NVIDIA countered that its customers depreciate GPUs over four to six years based on real-world use. Market prices are less forgiving than either: Silicon Data found used H100s listed at 45–55% of the price of a new one about three years after launch. Buildings financed over two decades will house several generations of chips. Debt secured on the chips themselves has far less time.

**Prices have already fallen once, and supply is catching up.** The bears also note that the price recovery the bulls cite followed a steep decline. Between early 2023 and October 2025, the midpoint of the one-year H100 contract range fell by about 44%. Goldman Sachs Research's December 2025 base case has data center occupancy peaking around 93% in 2026 and easing after that as new supply arrives.

**Credit markets are growing more cautious.** The strain is starting to show among lenders as well. Investor requests to redeem shares in non-traded business development companies, which give individual investors access to private credit, reached a record 12.4% of net assets in the second quarter of 2026, according to Stanger. Managers met 38% of them, returning $5.9 billion. Blue Owl Credit Income Corp received requests for $3.1 billion, or 16.8% of its shares, in the third quarter and repurchased only up to its 5% quarterly limit. Funds managed by the same firm own 80% of Hyperion. Regulators are watching the links. The US Office of Financial Research found in March 2026 that exposures between banks and private credit funds are the main channel through which risk could spread, and the Financial Stability Board noted in May that private credit at its current size has not been tested in a severe downturn. Public credit is repricing too. S&P cut Oracle to BBB−, one notch above junk, in July 2026, and Oracle's five-year credit default swaps reached about 203 basis points that month, the highest since the end of 2008.

Not all of this repricing is a warning. A company that moves from selling software to building infrastructure has a different credit profile, and its borrowing costs should reflect that. The bear case is ultimately a question about whether the adjustment stays orderly. When the evidence on both sides is this strong, history is a useful guide to how such adjustments have gone before.

## History shows where the losses landed last time

The telecom boom of the late 1990s is the closest precedent, and it is useful less because it will repeat than because it shows who ended up bearing the losses. US investment in communications equipment more than doubled, from about $62 billion a year in early 1996 to over $135 billion by late 2000, in 1996 dollars. Andrew Odlyzko estimated that US fiber capacity grew around 500-fold between the end of 1997 and the end of 2001, while demand grew about fourfold. In 2002, only 3.9% of the long-haul fiber running through Chicago was lit, according to TeleGeography data.

When the gap between capacity and demand closed, it closed on the financiers. The market value of S&P telecommunications companies fell by roughly $700 billion between 2000 and 2002. US bank regulators' review of large syndicated loans found classified telecom and cable loans rising from $2.1 billion in 2000 to $36.3 billion in 2002, 27% of commitments. Global Crossing filed for bankruptcy in January 2002 and WorldCom, listing over $107 billion of assets, in July. Much of the fiber was eventually used as internet traffic grew. It outlived many of the companies and lenders that paid for it.

The parallel is instructive, but three differences stand out today:

1. **Capacity is tight, not idle.** In 1997–2001, fiber capacity grew about 500-fold while demand grew about fourfold. Today, North American data center vacancy is about 1% and GPU rents have recovered.
2. **The biggest spenders are among the most profitable companies in the world.** Their spending is now outrunning their cash flow, but they started from far stronger positions than most 1990s carriers.
3. **GPUs last years, not decades.** An overbuild would clear faster than fiber did. But lenders would have much less time for demand to catch up before their collateral lost most of its value.

The first two differences favor the bulls. The third cuts the other way for anyone lending against chips, and it points to a distinction the debate often misses.

## Debt on buildings and debt on chips carry different risks

Much of the bubble debate treats AI infrastructure as one asset. For a lender it is at least two, and they behave very differently in a downturn.

**Building and power debt** finances long-lived assets: land, grid connections, shells and cooling. It is often backed by leases to investment-grade tenants. Hyperion is an example. Its notes run to 2049 and amortize over that period, supported by Meta's lease payments and residual value guarantee. The main risks are the tenant's credit and whether the tenant stays after the initial term.

**GPU debt** finances hardware with an economic life measured in years, and its risks are of a different kind. Rents can fall by 40% or more, and resale values fall as new generations arrive. Contracts are often shorter than the debt: CoreWeave's August 2026 facility runs about five years against customer contracts averaging about three. When conditions turn, the cash flow and the collateral weaken together.

The sharpest mismatch in the bubble debate is therefore long-dated money funding short-lived hardware. That is where structure matters most, and it is the part of the market OpenSilicon works in. Because the risk concentrates there, the useful task is to watch the signals that would show conditions turning.

## A scorecard shows which way conditions are moving

Rather than forecasting which side wins, we track the indicators on which the two cases make different predictions. The table below sets out what each side would expect to see.

| Indicator | A bull would expect | A bear would expect |
|---|---|---|
| Contract rents, by GPU model and term | Stable or rising as inference grows | Falling as new supply and new generations arrive |
| Paid occupancy and contract length | High occupancy, longer contracts | More spot capacity, shorter contracts |
| Cloud revenue and contracted backlog | Growing at least as fast as capital spending | Slowing while spending keeps rising |
| Secondary GPU prices | Holding up after each new generation | Falling sharply after each new generation |
| Pricing of GPU-backed debt | Spreads narrowing as the asset class matures | Spreads widening, lenders demanding more protection |
| Private credit flows | Redemptions easing, lending steady | Redemptions gated, lenders pulling back |
| Hyperscaler capital spending | Funded mainly from operating cash flow | Funded increasingly with debt and leases |

No single indicator settles the question. Several moving the same way at once would. A scorecard tells us which way the evidence is leaning; it does not remove the need to lend in a way that survives either outcome.

## Lending that survives the bear case

We do not need to know whether AI infrastructure is a bubble to lend against it sensibly. We need loans that hold up if the bears turn out to be right. In practice, that means:

- **Sizing to stressed cash flow**, not to a continued recovery in rental prices.
- **Repaying within the contract and the hardware's useful life**, rather than leaving a large balance to be refinanced at maturity.
- **Not relying on refinancing**, which tends to disappear exactly when it is needed.
- **Diversifying** across GPU generations, customers and sites, so that one repricing does not affect the whole portfolio.
- **Using conservative recovery values**, because hardware prices and rental prices tend to fall together.

Each principle answers one of the bear arguments above. If the bulls are right, loans structured this way are repaid early and comfortably. If the bears are right, they are the loans that still get repaid.

## Summary

Both the bull and bear cases rest on real evidence. Demand is turning into revenue, while spending and borrowing are growing faster than proven profits and some of the money circulates among a small group of firms. For a lender, the useful question is whether an asset repays before its contract, or its usefulness, runs out.

A lender's question is narrower than an investor's because its upside is capped at repayment. The bulls can show usage, revenue, long contracts, tight capacity and recovering rents. The bears can show spending outrunning cash flow, a surge in borrowing and off-balance-sheet commitments, vendor financing that links the largest players, hardware that ages faster than the debt against it and early signs of caution in credit markets. The telecom boom shows that infrastructure can be useful and still ruin its financiers, and the most important difference today, the short life of GPUs, matters most to lenders against chips. That is why debt on buildings and debt on chips should be judged differently, why we track a scorecard rather than make a call, and why we lend only on terms that survive the bear case.

The bubble question may not be settled for years. A lender does not need it to be. It needs each loan to be repaid on schedule whichever side turns out to be right.
